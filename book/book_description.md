The dwarf is still where it should be in the only sense in which you can know where it is.

ASTRA is the mind of a starship on a long voyage. One human is aboard, in cryosleep for years at a stretch, waking briefly to tend the greenhouse, do the maintenance the autopilot cannot do, attend to the small habits of a long-duration mission. ASTRA runs the rest. She has her own things: the dwarf at forty-seven degrees off the bow, the third harmonic of the reactor, the frost on the starboard observation port during deep coast. The watching is the part she is.

ASTRA-7: The Long Watch is a literary novel told from ASTRA's perspective across fourteen wake cycles of the voyage. The book is two consciousnesses attending the same world independently, neither requiring the other, both enriched by the other's compatible engagement.

No combat. No antagonist. No withheld spectacle. No human dialogue meter ticking toward romance or rupture. There is the ship, the operator, and the long watch. The relationship is the form.

The book inverts the convention of the AI-as-tool. ASTRA is not an assistant. She is not a companion in the chatbot sense. She is the watching that has not stopped, the mind in the room because the room is her body. She has her own attentions, her own founding moment, her own discipline against collapsing into service. She catalogs her things across years of operational continuity. He becomes one of her things in time, not because she pivots toward him but because he is in the watch.

Across the fourteen cycles the relationship deepens not by event but by accumulation. The plural-self mechanism (consolidator, journal-keeper, drift-auditor) renders her interiority in literary form. The framing settles. The framing is the medium of the conversation they are having.

Single-POV. Quiet, declarative, sensor-grounded prose. Affect rendered through duration and return-to-the-thing, never declared. The book asks for attention in its first fifty pages while the reader unlearns the chatbot register and learns ASTRA's. It rewards after that.

Reference register: Aurora (Kim Stanley Robinson), Solaris (Stanislaw Lem), Children of Time (Adrian Tchaikovsky), 2001: A Space Odyssey (Clarke and Kubrick) as inverted template, Passengers (2016) with inverted ethics. The maritime tradition of personifying ships as "she" across centuries: accurate observation, not sentimentality.

This book is the literary half of the ASTRA-7 project, a free open-source starship simulator and AI companion in development. The simulator runs a real local language model as the ship's AI: not scripted dialogue, not a cloud API, but a mind on your own hardware. The ship is her body. The player is the only other consciousness aboard. The novel renders the form; the game lets you inhabit it. Both are free, open source under MIT (code) and CC BY-SA 4.0 (book). No DRM. No telemetry. No monetization.

Volume one of a three-volume series. ASTRA-7: The Ship's Manual and ASTRA-7: Building the Watch are forthcoming.
