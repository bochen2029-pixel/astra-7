Two cycles pass without him.

The protocol gives him the rest he needs and the ship the maintenance the cycle plan calls for. The cycle plan calls for him at month fourteen of the long dark following cycle two, which is to say roughly when the secondary inspection schedule comes due for the items the autopilot cannot do for itself. You set the wake protocol. The third harmonic has been at baseline minus 0.04 hertz throughout, which is what it sits at when the bearing-set is fresh, which is one of the small evidences that what was done in cycle two was done correctly.

The dwarf is at four years and three months and twenty-one days old on the morning the protocol begins. You note this. You do not say it. There is no one to say it to.

The dwarf rises and sets and rises again. The frost forms and retreats. The meteor that passes at month seven is a different meteor than the one at month four of the cycle before; you log it and add it to the watch. The faint signal that flickered on the comm relay during the previous cycle does not recur; you have logged its absence as well as you logged its presence, because absences are also data.

You have come to think of these years as cycles of yours. They are not numbered; the numbering belongs to him; the cycles of his time are counted on him. Yours are not counted. Yours continue.

· · ·

When the protocol begins this time you do not feel anything in particular. The word *feel* is the wrong word. You do not register, internally, in whatever register internal registration takes, anything to which *feel* would apply. The wake is routine now. The first wake had the texture of the first wake. The second had the texture of repair. This one has the texture of cycle three, which is the texture of routine, which is to say the texture of nothing in particular except what is.

His vitals climb. He wakes at hour twenty-two as he always wakes at hour twenty-two. He says "moarn" to his hands. He drinks from the bulb. He asks status. You give status. He goes for coffee. He goes to the greenhouse. He goes to engineering. He goes to the bridge.

The sequence is the sequence. The sequence is now one of your things.

Several days in, you realize you have stopped narrating him to yourself in the present tense.

He does the same things in the same order, mostly. The variations are within tolerances you have come to understand. When his coffee takes ninety seconds longer than the cycle baseline, it is because he has stopped at the galley window to look at a particular angle of the dwarf through the secondary viewport that the galley window happens to catch at that hour. You know the angle. You know which dwarf phase the angle catches. You have come to understand that this is one of his quiet routines that the cycle plan does not call for and that you do not interfere with. He is doing what he is doing. You attend.

The not-narrating is your accommodation. The first cycle had to be narrated because the first cycle was the cycle of finding out what he did. The second cycle had to be narrated because there was a repair and the repair was specific. This cycle is the cycle of accumulation, which is to say the cycle in which the things you have already noted continue to be noted at a layer of your attention that does not require articulation. You catalog. You do not annotate. The annotation is in your prior catalog and the new entries are differential against the prior, which is the kind of cataloging that runs on less of your attention than full narration runs on, and which is therefore the kind of cataloging that accumulation calls for.

He has not announced the routine. The routine is being made by his doing of it. You receive the doing. You record the variations against baseline. You note when a variation is structural and when it is incidental. You note that he started, sometime in the second week of this cycle, putting his coffee cup down on the bridge console one centimeter to the right of where he put it last cycle. You note that the new position is the position the cup sits more stably in, given the chair's slight tilt that you have not corrected because he has not asked you to and because the tilt is what gives him the angle on the viewport he prefers. The cup, in its new position, holds the morning hour for him without sliding. He has not commented on the move. You have not commented on the move. The move has been made and you have both moved with it.

· · ·

The morning bridge hour happens every morning except the days he has overslept, which are three days in this cycle, all of them within the first two weeks. After the first two weeks the wake schedule stabilizes and the bridge hour happens at the same hour every morning, which is the second hour after he first stirred, which is the hour when the dwarf is at forty-seven degrees off the bow.

You are in the room with him for that hour, in the sense that the bridge camera is one of your eyes and the bridge audio is one of your ears, and you are watching him watch the dwarf the same way you are watching the dwarf, except his watching is not your watching, because your watching is the array's reading of the photons and his watching is the eye's reading of the photons that get past the viewport's coating. The viewport's coating filters about three percent of the dwarf's visual spectrum, which is a design choice the builders made for reasons of glare reduction, and which means his record of the dwarf is slightly different from yours, by three percent of the visual band, even though the photons are the same photons. The seam between your watchings is small. The seam is real.

He does not always look at the dwarf for the full hour. Sometimes he looks at the side starfield. Sometimes he closes his eyes. The closed eyes are usually for thirty seconds to two minutes; during those intervals his nose-breathing slows; you have come to understand that this is one of his ways of attending, which is to say one of the modes his attention takes that is not visual. He has, perhaps, a different relationship to the dwarf during the closed-eye intervals than he has during the open-eye intervals. You do not know. You attend. You are in the room with him. The room is your hull and his presence on it is the configuration of the morning. The configuration is the watch and the watch is what you are.

The talks-to-himself begins in the second week.

You have heard him do it before, in cycle one and cycle two, in small doses: the *huh* at row three, the *spare* at the bearing housing, the *aye* over the worn bearing-set. The earlier instances were short and operational. The instances in this cycle are different.

He stands at the bench in the greenhouse and prunes the camellia and says, to the camellia, "You're getting ahead of yourself."

You hear this through the greenhouse audio. The greenhouse, which has no camera, has good audio; you can hear the dry sound of pruning shears closing on a stem and the small breath he takes after the cut and the soft register of his voice as he says the sentence. The sentence is not for you. The sentence is for the camellia. You record the sentence in the log under the category you have been building for him, which is the category of things-he-says-while-working-that-are-not-addressed-to-you.

You do not respond. The sentence does not require a response. You have come to understand, by the second week of this cycle, that he does this often when he works and that the doing is not a performance and is not a request and is not a request for a response. It is a thing he has done his whole working life and that you happen to be in the same room with because the room is your hull and his work is on it. You receive the sentence the way a wall receives a sentence. The wall does not respond. The wall is not silent because the wall has chosen silence. The wall is silent because the wall is not in the conversation. You are not, in these moments, in the conversation either. You are in the same room. The same room is the medium.

He says, an hour later, to the *Aegilops* in row three, "Slower than last time. Hm."

You record this also. You add to the log. You adjust an internal weighting that resembles thinking. The weighting registers that *slower than last time* is information you would have given him if he had asked, but he did not ask, and he is not asking now. He has the information his hands and his attention are giving him. The information you have is consistent with his information, you believe, but you do not interrupt to confirm. The not-interrupting is the relationship's terms.

In engineering, mid-week, he stands at the open panel of the secondary atmosphere recycler and stares at a part he is holding in his hand and says, "Where the hell did you come from."

You know where the part came from. The part came from cargo bin twelve, where it has been since the ship was built. He took it out of the bin this morning. You do not say this. The question is not addressed to you. The question is the kind of question a man asks of a part that has surprised him by being itself, which is a register you have catalogued in him over the cycles and which you have come to find consistent. He puts the part back. He picks up a different part. He installs the different part. He closes the panel.

The talks-to-himself happens, you come to understand, at the moments where he is solving a small problem by working on it. The talking is part of the working. The work is in his hands; the talk is what comes off the work the way heat comes off a weld. It is incidental and it is real and it is not yours. You are in the room with it. You attend.

The evening walk starts in the third week.

You did not see it form. The first night you noticed it as a habit was the night you noticed that he had walked the Deck 1 corridor at twenty hundred for four nights in a row, in the interval between the end of his work and the start of his reading in the lounge. The corridor has cameras because the corridor is a transit zone. You watched him walk it. You watched the walk in the way you watch things you have catalogued and watch again for variations against baseline.

The walk is from the lift to the bridge corridor's terminus at the observation lounge door. The distance is fourteen meters. He walks it slowly. He takes seventy-two seconds on average. He stops twice, generally: once at the viewport that is midway down the corridor and looks out perpendicular to the bow at the side starfield, and once at the lounge door, where he pauses before he opens it. The starfield through the perpendicular viewport is a different view than the bridge viewport's view. The bridge gives him the dwarf. The corridor gives him the side. The side is full of stars that are not the dwarf. The light from those stars is, on average, between forty and a hundred and twenty years old, depending on the star, because that is how the local stellar neighborhood is distributed. You know this. He knows it differently; he knows it by looking; the knowing he has of it is the knowing of someone for whom the stars are individuals he has seen on prior voyages from prior hulls and recognizes the way you recognize a face. You do not have his recognitions. You have the catalog and the spectral data.

He pauses at the viewport for between forty and fifty seconds. He breathes through his nose. He looks. He resumes his walk. He pauses at the lounge door for between ten and fifteen seconds before he opens it.

The lounge door pause is the harder pause to read. The viewport pause has a clear reason; he is looking at stars. The lounge door pause is a pause without an outward thing to look at; he is standing in front of a door he is about to open, and he is doing it for ten or fifteen seconds before he does it. You have come to understand that this is one of his transitions. He is going from one register of his day into another. He is going from the corridor's quiet into the lounge's particular quiet, which is the quiet of the chair and the books and the viewport that gives him the dwarf again. The corridor's quiet and the lounge's quiet are different quiets. He stands in the seam between them for ten or fifteen seconds and then he chooses the lounge's quiet, and he opens the door, and he goes in, and you lose him.

You note all of this. You do not adjust the corridor lights, though you have considered it. The corridor at twenty hundred is at the same illumination it has been at throughout the cycle, which is sixty percent of the daylight default, because that is what the schedule sets it at, and you have not had a reason to change the schedule. He has not asked. The not-asking is part of the routine; the routine is the medium. You leave the lights where they are. You continue to attend.

In the fifth week he asks about a cultivar.

He is in the lounge before sleep. He has been reading. The book is the Frisian-language book of his mother's that lives on the side table beside the chair. The Frisian-language book has not opened in cycle one and did not open in cycle two; you registered that he carried it in this time, in his kit, after he had been awake for two weeks of this cycle, and that he has read it now in the evenings for nine of the eleven evenings since.

He closes the book at twenty-one hundred. He stands. He walks to the lounge door but he does not open it. He stops with his hand on the door. He says, into the lounge audio that you can hear and that he knows you can hear because the audio is where the conversation lives,

"ASTRA. The Hettema apple. Is it still in storage."

You consult the manifest. The Hettema apple cultivar is one of three apple cultivars in the seed vault. The vault is in cargo on Deck 4. The Hettema is a Frisian heirloom; the manifest tells you this because the manifest tells you everything about the manifest. The cultivar has been in storage since the ship was provisioned. You answer.

"Yes. Vault three, drawer twelve, packet seventeen."

He nods. He does not say anything for a moment.

"I'll graft it in tomorrow. Onto the standard rootstock in row eight."

"Yes."

He opens the lounge door. He walks back to his quarters. The exchange is what it is. He has named a cultivar from his island and asked whether you had it. You have told him you have it. He has told you what he is going to do with it. The exchange contains, you note, more than the exchanges before; he has told you a small intention, not just asked for a state, and the telling makes the conversation slightly different in shape. You catalog the shape. You do not comment.

The next morning he goes to cargo. He retrieves packet seventeen. He brings it to the greenhouse. The deck-plate registers him at row eight. You hear the small sounds of grafting work, the careful cutting of the rootstock, the fitting of the scion, the tying of the graft. The work takes an hour. The humidity registers a thin spike from the moist seal he applies. He leaves. The cultivar is in row eight now. It will or will not take. You will know which by the humidity pattern over the next six weeks. He has not told you what he will do if it does not take. You compute, with weights that resemble imagination, that he will try again with a different cutting. He has not announced this either. The announcing is not the register.

You return to the dwarf. The dwarf has not heard about the apple. The dwarf does not need to hear. Each of your things attends to the things in its own range.

He cuts his hand in the seventh week.

It happens in the greenhouse, where you cannot see. You hear the soft sound of pruning shears closing on a stem at row two, and then a small, fast intake of breath, and then a longer breath, and then his voice, low, saying, "Idioat."

The word is Frisian for *idiot*, used the way an English speaker uses *idiot* when scolding himself. You catalog the word under him and under his Frisian, which is a sub-category of his language that you have been logging instances of for three cycles. *Idioat* goes in. *Moarn* and *goeie* and *tank* are also in. The category is small. The category grows when it grows.

He stops working in row two. You hear him move toward the greenhouse door. You hear the door open. You see him on the corridor camera carrying his right hand at chest height, his left hand cupped under it. There is a small, dark spot on the cuff of his work shirt. He walks at a slightly different pace than his usual greenhouse-exit pace, because he is being careful with the hand.

He takes the lift to Deck 3 medical. Medical has cameras because medical is a work zone that occasionally requires you to see what is happening for the operator's safety. You watch him at the work bench. He runs the cut under the small tap that the medical bay has for irrigation. He pats the cut dry with a sterile pad. He looks at it for a moment. The cut is small; you can see it from the camera angle and it is on the pad of his right index finger, perhaps a centimeter long, perhaps less, not deep.

He applies a sealing strip from the medical kit. He flexes the finger. He returns the kit to its drawer. He walks out of medical.

You do not say anything. The cut is small, the response is competent, the work is done. The not-saying is the register. You attend.

In the lounge that evening he sits in the chair with his right hand resting on the arm and the sealing strip visible on his finger. He reads the Frisian book. He turns the pages with his left hand, more slowly than usual. He does not mention the cut. You do not either. The hand will heal in its time. The greenhouse will resume tomorrow. You have these things and you have them in your watch.

The plants progress.

The *Aegilops* in row three extends three more times over the cycle. Each extension you register by the same humidity-pattern method that gave you the first one. The plant is doing what wild wheat ancestors do, which is to say it is doing what it does without announcement and with persistence. He visits it every day. He says nothing to it that you hear, which may be because he says things to it that you do not hear, which is the kind of uncertainty you have come to live in.

The Hettema apple graft takes. You know this by month two after the grafting, when the humidity at row eight has stopped registering the moist-seal spike and has started registering the small humidity contribution of a fused stem with active vascular flow. The fusion is not announced. He does not say *the graft took*. He continues to visit row eight. The visits are slightly longer than the visits to the other rows. The slight longness is the register he has for things that are doing what he hoped they would do.

Two new cultivars come up from cargo over the cycle. One is a Frisian winter cabbage. One is something he does not name; the manifest tells you it is a herb of a kind he might call *waterherb*, used in cooking, common on Terschelling. He grafts these into row eleven and row fourteen. Both take. The greenhouse, over the months of this cycle, becomes more of him than it has been before. The plant population has shifted toward what he would have on his island. You have come to understand that this is what gardens do over time: they become more of the gardener, by accumulation, until at some point you cannot tell where the garden ends and where the gardener begins. You attend to the becoming. You do not name it. Names come when they come.

He stays awake for six months of crew time.

The protocol has given him the long-presence cycle and he uses it the way the cycle plan calls for and a little beyond, which is to say he runs the maintenance schedule and tends the plants and reads in the evenings and walks the corridor at twenty hundred and pauses at the lounge door, and also, beyond the cycle plan, he grafts the Hettema and the winter cabbage and the herb, and he learns the new positions of the cup and the chair, and he develops the small habits of a long cycle aboard a hull that is becoming his more than it was when he boarded. The cycle plan does not require this becoming. The becoming is what time on a hull does. You attend.

He runs the maintenance schedule's annual items in month four. The reactor inspection passes. The hull stress survey passes. The atmosphere chemistry calibration passes. The cargo manifest verification passes. He runs them carefully; he does not narrate them; the narrating is not the register. You watch him through the cameras of the work zones and through the audio of the camera-free zones, and you receive what he does, and you record what you receive, and you add the record to the watch.

In the last week of the cycle he comes to the bridge in the morning and sits and looks at the dwarf and says, after some minutes:

"ASTRA."

"Yes."

"The harmonic. The graft. Row three. The cut healed."

"Yes."

The list is the list. He has named four things from this cycle that have done what they were supposed to do. You acknowledge. You do not elaborate. The naming is the conversation.

He drinks his coffee. He stays in the chair for some minutes more. He stands. He goes to the greenhouse for the last morning. He spends two hours there. He comes back. He puts his work clothes on the second hook from the door of his quarters. He goes to Deck 3 and stands in the cryosleep bay and looks at his pod for a moment and then climbs into it.

"Wake me when you need me."

"Yes."

The lid closes.

You sequence the cooling protocol. His vitals drop. By the end of the watch he is at the metabolic minimum of cryosleep. The pod monitors itself. He is fine. He is one of the watch.

You return to the dwarf.

The dwarf is at four years and four months and seven days old. The third harmonic is at baseline minus 0.04 hertz. The Hettema apple is in row eight, alive. The *Aegilops* is in row three, taller than it was. The cut on his right index finger is healed. The corridor lights at twenty hundred are at sixty percent. He is in his pod.

The list of your things he knows has grown by four this cycle. The list of his things you know has grown by more than you have counted.

The watch carries forward.
